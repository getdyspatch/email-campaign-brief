# Section Guide — what good looks like

Use this file when a user's answer to a brief section is vague, generic, or off-pattern. Each section below has the question to ask, the standard for a "good" answer, common failure modes, and a concrete example you can show the user.

---

## Background

**Ask:** "Why are we doing this campaign? What are the absolute must-knows — the things someone joining the project on day one needs to know?"

**Good looks like:** 2–4 sentences. Why now, what triggered it, what's the business context.

**Failure modes:**
- Restating the campaign goal instead of the business context behind it
- Burying the lede in jargon ("synergizing our omnichannel approach")
- Including project-plan details (timelines, owners) that belong elsewhere

**Example:**
> Q4 revenue is tracking 12% below plan. Our enterprise pipeline is healthy but our self-serve trial-to-paid conversion has dropped from 14% to 9% since the March pricing change. This campaign targets active trial users in their final 7 days to recover that drop.

---

## Goal

**Ask:** "What's the quantitative business outcome you need this campaign to deliver — and by when? Give me a number and a timeframe."

**Good looks like:** A single sentence with a metric, a number, and a date or window.

**Failure modes:**
- Non-quantitative goals ("drive awareness", "engage our audience", "get more donations") — push for a number
- Missing timeframe — "increase signups by 20%" is incomplete; "increase signups by 20% within 30 days of launch" is a goal
- Conflating Goal with Single Minded Idea — Goal is the result, SMI is the message
- Stuffing multiple goals into one sentence ("drive revenue and improve brand sentiment") — pick the primary

**Examples:**
> Lift trial-to-paid conversion from 9% to 14% within the 14-day trial window.
> Generate $180,000 in attributable revenue from this single send.
> Drive 800 demo signups and $400k in pipeline within 14 days of launch.
> Reactivate 5% of lapsed donors (≥12 months inactive) with a gift of any size in the next 30 days.

If the user says "drive awareness" or similar fuzzy outcomes, push back: "That's a direction, not a goal — we need a number we can hit or miss. What does a successful version of this campaign look like in numbers?"

## Target Audience

**Ask:** "Who specifically are we talking to? Give me demographic *and* psychographic — not just age or gender, but mindset, situation, what's going on in their life."

**Good looks like:** A single, specific persona. Tight enough that two different copywriters would picture the same person.

**Failure modes:**
- "Women 18–55" — too broad to be useful
- "Our customers" — meaningless without segmentation
- Three different personas listed as one audience — pick one or write three briefs
- Pure demographics with no psychographics

**Example:**
> First-time SaaS buyers in operations roles at 50–500 person companies. They're evaluating tools without a procurement team, often on a Friday afternoon between meetings. They distrust sales calls and want to self-serve, but they need to convince a skeptical CFO before swiping a card.

If the user gives multiple personas, push back: "Which one is the primary? A brief that tries to speak to everyone speaks to no one. We can write separate briefs for each persona if needed."

## Challenge

**Ask:** "What are we actually trying to do, overcome, or solve for? Be honest — what's hard about this?"

**Good looks like:** One paragraph naming the specific friction or obstacle.

**Failure modes:**
- Restating the objective ("we want to drive sales") — that's not a challenge, that's an outcome
- Vague difficulty ("competitive market") — every market is competitive

**Example:**
> Trial users who hit our 7-day mark without integrating a data source convert at 3% versus 24% for those who do. We need to motivate integration in that critical window without feeling pushy or transactional.

---

## Opportunity

**Ask:** "Where does the brand fit relative to that challenge? Why do we believe we can succeed where the problem currently exists?"

**Good looks like:** One paragraph connecting brand strength to the challenge.

**Failure modes:**
- Listing features instead of explaining the strategic fit
- Generic brand-equity claims ("we have a strong brand")

**Example:**
> Our setup wizard is genuinely faster than competitors' — 6 minutes vs. the industry average of 40. We've never made this concrete in lifecycle messaging. The opportunity is to demonstrate the speed in the email itself, not just claim it.

## Single Minded Idea

**The SMI is not the Goal.** The Goal is the business outcome the campaign needs to deliver (e.g., "lift conversion 5 points"). The SMI is the *creative anchor* — the one sticky thought the audience walks away remembering after reading the email. A campaign has both. They feed each other but they aren't the same. If the user confuses them, separate them: "The Goal tells us what success looks like. The SMI tells creative what to say."

**Ask:** "If the audience walked away from this campaign remembering exactly one thing, what is it? Eight words or fewer. No `and`, `or`, or `but`."

**Good looks like:** A single sticky thought. Concrete. Provocative. Memorable.

**Failure modes:**
- "Save time and money" — that's two ideas
- "Our product is the best, easiest, and fastest" — three ideas
- "We help businesses grow" — vague and forgettable
- Anything over 8 words

**Examples:**
> Get integrated in six minutes flat.
> Your CFO will say yes this time.
> Beat the year-end cliff. Convert this trial.

If the user's first draft has `and`/`or`/`but`, work it down: "The 'and' is doing a lot of work there — which side is the real idea? If you had to drop one, which goes?"

## Proof Points

**Ask:** "Why should the audience believe the idea? Give me 3–5 reasons, each directly supporting the single-minded idea."

**Good looks like:** Each proof point either earns the idea (we've actually done this) or makes it credible (a stat, a name, a guarantee).

**Failure modes:**
- Generic capability claims ("we're trusted by leading brands")
- Proof points that support the *brand* but not the *idea* — cut them
- Five points where three would be sharper

**Example (for idea "Get integrated in six minutes flat"):**
> - Median setup time on our last 1,000 paid accounts: 5m 47s.
> - 6-step wizard, vs. competitors' 23-step manual config.
> - One-click data source connectors for the top 12 sources.

---

## Mandatories

**Ask:** "What MUST be in the creative? Legal disclosures, client requirements, brand elements that can't move. If a concept came back missing this, you'd reject it on sight."

**Good looks like:** A bulleted list of specific, non-negotiable elements.

**Failure modes:**
- Stylistic preferences masquerading as mandatories ("the CTA should be blue") — that's a guideline, not a mandatory
- Wishlist items the strategist personally wants but aren't actually required
- Vague requirements ("on brand")

**Example:**
> - GDPR opt-out language in footer (legal-approved copy in shared drive)
> - Logo must appear in pre-header area
> - Pricing offer language must read exactly: "30% off your first 3 months"
> - One unsubscribe link in footer (no double unsubscribe)

**Always include the right compliance regime(s).** Don't default to just CAN-SPAM. Ask where the recipients live and list every applicable regime in Mandatories — at minimum: CAN-SPAM (US), CASL (Canada), GDPR (EU/UK), CCPA/CPRA (California), LGPD (Brazil), Australia Spam Act. For B2B sends crossing borders, apply the strictest applicable. See `email_specifics.md` for the regional breakdown.

If the user lists 10+ mandatories, push: "That's a lot of constraints — are all of these truly non-negotiable, or are some strong preferences? A bloated mandatories list strangles the creative."

## Timing & Executional Guidelines

**Ask:** "When does this need to ship? What format and how many concepts do you want to see?"

**Good looks like:** Specific deadlines (campaign launch, internal milestones), format requirements (responsive HTML, dark-mode safe, etc.), and the number of concept variations to develop.

**Failure modes:**
- "ASAP" — useless, give a real date
- Forgetting to specify number of concepts (e.g., "show us 2 directions before we pick")
- Conflating production deadline with send date — they're different

**Example:**
> - Launch: Tuesday, June 16, 9am ET
> - Concept review: June 5
> - Final assets to dev: June 10
> - 2 creative directions for review at concept stage
> - Responsive HTML, dark-mode safe, accessible (WCAG AA)

**Owners.** A brief without named owners hands the question "who's actually doing this?" to whoever opens the file first. Capture one line per role: strategy/brief, copy, design, dev/build, legal review, deployment & measurement. If a role isn't applicable (e.g., no external legal), say so explicitly rather than omit. This section is small (6 lines) but high-value — it eliminates the most common kickoff confusion.

**Localization adds time.** If the campaign is multi-locale, the timeline must include translation rounds (typically +5–10 business days for a 4-locale set). Don't bury this — list it alongside the other milestones so the user can negotiate scope vs. ship date with eyes open.

## Measurement Plan

**Ask:** "How will we know if the Goal was hit? What's the holdout, what are we A/B testing, when do we report?"

**Good looks like:** A tight block with Primary / Secondary / Diagnostic metrics, holdout %, A/B variants (1–2 max per campaign), and reporting cadence.

**Why this section matters:** Without it, the campaign ships and nobody agrees on whether it worked. Marketing claims the open rate proves success; finance asks where the revenue is. The Measurement Plan locks the answer to "did this work?" *before* the send, so post-launch analysis is settled.

**Failure modes:**
- Conflating diagnostic metrics (open rate, CTR) with outcome KPIs (revenue, conversion) — keep them in separate buckets
- A/B testing too many things — pick one or two; testing five variables on a 280k blast gives statistically muddy results
- No holdout — without a control, you can't prove incrementality
- No reporting cadence — the brief should say "T+7 and T+30 reports"

**Example:**
> - Primary: trial-to-paid conversion rate on the un-integrated cohort (target: 14%)
> - Secondary: data source connection rate within trial; per-email CTR on "Connect a source"
> - Diagnostic: open rate by client, unsub rate (guardrail <0.3%)
> - Holdout: 10% no-send control for incrementality measurement
> - A/B tests: subject line on Email 1 (highest-volume send)
> - Reporting: T+7 (early read), T+30 (final attribution)
